Student content creation at IIT Delhi: a qualitative case study

Avneel, Vanshija and Preeti

Seven IIT Delhi students who make videos for YouTube and Instagram told us how they decide what to film, what to leave out, and whom they make it for. This page follows the study from its first question to its findings.

Part 1

Research question

The question was revised three times, each version tested against Agee's (2009) four faults: a yes/no question, one too broad, one that leads, one that packs several questions together.

version 1
How do student vloggers construct and perform an “IIT Delhi student” identity for online audiences?
version 2
What role does the IIT Delhi name play in how student creators build and present their channels?
version 3
How do IIT Delhi student content creators produce and use representations of the Institution for online audiences?
final
How do IIT Delhi student content creators develop and present their online content?
1. What informs the selection of content creators choose to produce and share?
2. How does affiliation with IIT Delhi feature in the creation and presentation of their content?
3. How do creators understand and engage with their online audiences?
Version 1 · 6 November

An identity we had assumed

not yes/nonot too broadleadingborderline packed

The question took for granted that an “IIT Delhi identity” exists and is performed. The first transcripts did not show that. Creators used the name to reach people and spoke of moving away from it. Most also make Instagram reels, not vlogs.

Version 2

Open, but narrow

not yes/nonot too broadborderline leadingnot packed

“What role” left room for the answer “very little”, which one participant gave. But much of the data on audiences, motives and omissions sat outside a question about the name alone.

Version 3

The assumption returns

not yes/nonot too broadleadingnot packed

“Use representations of the Institution” presumed that creators represent IIT Delhi. Several described their channels as being about themselves.

Final

Development and presentation

not yes/nonot too broadnot leadingnot packed

Open, and answerable from what we collected. Affiliation moves into a sub-question, where it can be found to matter, or not. Six sub-questions were reduced to three where two overlapped.

Part 2

Design

An instrumental case study (Creswell & Poth, 2018): one bounded case, studied for what it shows about content production, affiliation and audiences more widely.

Sampling

Seven creators

Purposive sampling: current IIT Delhi students who make content related to the institute on YouTube or Instagram (Creswell & Poth, 2018; Mills et al., 2010). They were found by keyword searches on both platforms and span B.Tech, M.Tech and PhD students. By the seventh interview, responses were largely recurring.

Approach

A bounded case

The case is bounded by one institution, one period and one practice. A narrative approach was the closest alternative, but our interest was a shared practice rather than individual life stories. Phenomenology, grounded theory and ethnography fit the question less well.

Interviews

Conversations on the lawns

Semi-structured, 35–45 minutes, in person, mostly on the Mechanical Lawns. The guide moved from open to specific and did not mention “IIT” until a late section, so participants described their work in their own terms first.

Public content

Reading the videos alongside

Each participant's public videos and reels were reviewed before and after the interview and read alongside the transcript. Several contradictions came to light this way.

Ethics

Consent and anonymity

Participants received an information sheet and signed a consent form with separate items for recording, quotation and use of their public videos. The invitation described the study plainly, without terms like “identity” that could shape answers. Because creators are publicly identifiable, they are referred to by number only and video content is paraphrased.

Finding 1 of 4

Institutional affiliation

All participants linked the IIT Delhi name to wider reach. They differed in how they used it, what it meant to them, and how long they meant to rely on it.

The name brings an audience

One participant had posted videos before joining IIT Delhi; they rarely passed 200 views.

“with IIT, this brand comes to your channel as well.”
P3, M.Tech

The scale of the difference

After joining, his videos reached millions. His explanation: “99% wants to know what is it that that 1% is getting.” Another participant described IIT-tagged posts with a motivational line outperforming her most carefully made videos.

The same name, opposite uses

P1 adds IIT Delhi to reach more people. P2 leaves it out when he wants to reach beyond the campus. The name's value depends on which audience a creator is aiming at.

What the name means to them

For P1 it is a responsibility (“I feel responsible for how I represent the institute”). For P2 it is a right (“I have every right to use it”). For P3 it is credibility that separates enrolled students from outsiders who film at the gate.

Relying on it less

P1 is adding personal content so the channel outlasts her degree. P3 pointed to an alumnus who still returns to campus to film (“I don't want to be that person”) and plans eventually to make his channel private. P4 said her channel would be no different elsewhere, though she began IIT Delhi content because “many people aspire to study here.”

Finding 2 of 4

Audiences

Participants mostly imagined audiences outside the institute.

Aspirants

The audience most participants named. P5 writes for “the middle bunch who need guidance” and answers their questions by email; one aspirant she helped now studies at an IIT.

Parents

P3's mother no longer asks whether he has eaten; she watches his videos. Other students' parents learn about campus events through him. P2 pictures “Indian parents” among his viewers.

“she's like, ‘Yeah, he's okay.’”
P3, M.Tech

Alumni, and those who did not get in

Graduates watch to revisit hostel life and placements; one video was shared in an alumni group. Students from other colleges and job seekers watch, in P3's words, to see “what we were missing”. (The word “longing” was the interviewer's first.)

Campus peers and family

Only P4 said most of her viewers are from IIT and her own age; she also reported the fewest expectations tied to IIT. Family is both audience and constraint: P1 posts carefully because relatives follow her account.

An imagined audience

Marwick and boyd (2011) describe the imagined audience a creator writes for. Two participants said they make content for themselves, then explained in detail which videos attract views and why. Knowledge of viewers came from analytics, comments and messages.

Finding 3 of 4

Aspiration

Most participants watched IIT content as aspirants. Now they make it for aspirants.

Watching

P2 watched creators during JEE preparation. P5 found a single IIT Delhi video at the time. P1 watched vlogs that presented IIT as the reward after exams.

“once I got into an IIT, I would be able to enjoy student life.”
P1, PhD

Getting in

For some, after many attempts. P3 describes seven to eight years, including two drop years. His message to viewers: “don't ever stop trying.”

Noticing a gap

P3 could find no account of how placements work. P5 found nothing on women's hostel life: “every other person is making around the boys' hostel.”

Creating

They make what they had wanted to see: motivational edits for admission season, campus tours, preparation strategy, placement week. The next aspirants watch.

What the cycle carries forward

P1 says the vlogs she watched showed festivals and nightlife, and she “did not fully understand the academic pressure involved”. Her own channel features food, festivals and facilities. One participant's channel name rejects this picture: getting into IIT “certainly doesn't make it any easier.” P4, a PhD entrant who never watched IIT content, is the negative case.

Finding 4 of 4

Content selection

The content participants said draws the most attention, criticism of the institute, is the content they most consistently withheld. Their reasons differed.

What performs

Participants named what draws views: festivals, food, placements, technology, anything unique to IIT Delhi. And above all, negative content about the institute.

“You say anything bad about the institute, people will come.”
P3, M.Tech

Institutional image

P1 and P3 both know criticism would get views; both avoid it. P1 supported a student protest but did not want viewers to blame the whole institute.

“I don't want to tarnish the image of the institute.”
P3, M.Tech

Discipline

P2 deleted reels showing curfew-breaking, removed a hostel video on a senior's advice, and holds back rule-breaking he finds funny for fear of the Disciplinary Committee.

External constraints

P3 removed a video on poverty in areas next to campus after an outside organisation threatened legal action, and does not film placement tests: “I think it's not right to post.”

Personal visibility

P5 keeps male friends and her own JEE story off camera, and avoids Instagram so campus peers will not see her as “a girl who just makes videos and does not study”. P4 avoids protest and conflict so she is not misjudged.

On screen and off screen

Goffman (1959) separates the front stage of performance from the back stage. Every participant described their content as an expression of themselves, and every one described something they adjust.

Part 6

Discussion

Read together, the findings describe content shaped by the creator's relationships with the institution, the audience and the platform.

Institution and creator

The institution supplies the name that brings an audience, and the limits on what can be shown. Those limits worked mostly through anticipation, the Disciplinary Committee and seniors' advice, rather than written rules. Creators took the name on as a responsibility or a right, and most planned to depend on it less.

Creator and audience

Creators wrote for an imagined audience mostly outside the institute. Aspirants asked for guidance, and creators who had been aspirants supplied it, sometimes beyond the video, by email and message.

Creator and platform

Metrics taught creators what works. Two participants explained success as luck while describing detailed lessons drawn from their metrics.

Platform and audience

What performs shapes what viewers come to expect. Negative content performs especially well, which places creators between what the platform rewards and what the institution and their own visibility allow.

Audience and institution

Through these videos, aspirants and parents form a picture of IIT Delhi curated by omission. Their demand for that picture is what gives the name its value on the platform.

Answer to the research questionIIT Delhi student creators develop their content by drawing on the institute's name to reach audiences outside it, mainly aspirants and their families. They present it within limits set by the institution, by the costs of being visible, and by what they think viewers expect of an IIT student. What the platforms reward most, criticism of the institute, is what they most consistently hold back. Their attachment to the name is real but provisional.

Implications

Information for aspirantsAspirants and parents learn about IIT life from student creators. Festivals, food, freedom and placements reach them; academic pressure, protests and rule-breaking mostly do not.
Institutional guidanceNo participant referred to a written policy on student content. Limits worked through anticipation. Clear guidance would reduce guesswork, including on filming other students without consent.
Unequal costs of visibilityOne woman participant described costs of being seen that no male participant mentioned. With one case this is a lead for further research, not a finding.
Part 7

Reflexivity

As IIT Delhi students studying IIT Delhi students, we were insiders and researchers at once.

Our first question assumed its answer

Version 1 took for granted an “IIT Delhi identity”. The early transcripts did not support it, and we revised the question and recorded why.

Insider familiarity

Shared campus knowledge made rapport easy, but we may not have asked about things that seemed too obvious to explain, such as hostel rules.

Interviewer wording

In one interview the interviewer suggested “a sense of longing” before the participant used it. Such passages are flagged and not treated as the participant's own framing.

A paired interview

Two participants are friends and collaborators, and one spoke during the other's interview. Their accounts are not fully independent.

The setting

Interviews took place in an open campus space with fellow students. Answers on protests, rules and criticism may have been more guarded than elsewhere.

Transcription

Transcripts were made with a transcription tool and cleaned. One line turned out to be the tool's note rather than the participant's words and was excluded. Quotations are checked against the recordings.

Our lens

Having begun with a question about performance, we may have read some accounts as adjustments where participants described themselves as natural. Each such reading was discussed before it was kept. Leaving some contradictions uninterpreted was also a decision.

Part 8

Limitations

  • Seven purposively selected creators; those who agreed may be the more confident or successful.
  • No viewers were interviewed, so accounts of audiences could not be checked.
  • Interviews capture descriptions of practice, not observed filming or editing.
  • Deleted or unpublished content could not be examined.
  • Search found only creators who name IIT Delhi publicly.
  • A small, public group at one institute is hard to anonymise fully.
